Two small habits that make English sound right.

Subject–verb agreement decides whether your verb matches its subject. Articles decide whether a noun is "a," "the," or nothing at all. Get these two right and your sentences instantly read as fluent. Every rule below has plain-English notes, correct-vs-incorrect examples, and a quick check.

Part One · 12 Rules

Subject–Verb Agreement

The core idea never changes: a singular subject takes a singular verb, and a plural subject takes a plural verb. Every rule below is really just this idea applied to a trickier sentence shape.

1

The basic rule

singular ↔ singular, plural ↔ plural

A singular subject (one person/thing) takes a singular verb (usually ending in -s). A plural subject takes a plural verb (no -s).

✓The dog barks at strangers.
✓The dogs bark at strangers.
✗The dog bark at strangers.

Quick check: "She (write/writes) every day."

Answer: writes — "she" is singular.
2

Subjects joined by "and"

usually plural — unless they name one single thing

Two subjects joined by and normally take a plural verb. Exception: if the two nouns together describe one single person, idea, or dish, use a singular verb.

✓Tom and Jerry are best friends. (two people → plural)
✓Bread and butter is my favorite breakfast. (one dish → singular)
✗The CEO and founder are arriving today. (same person)

Quick check: "Rice and curry (is/are) served here." (one dish)

Answer: is — treated as a single dish/idea.
3

Subjects joined by "or / nor"

the verb agrees with the nearer subject

With or, nor, either...or, or neither...nor, the verb agrees with whichever subject is closest to it — ignore the farther one.

✓Neither the teacher nor the students are ready.
✓Neither the students nor the teacher is ready.
✗Either my parents or my brother are coming. (brother is nearer → singular)

Quick check: "Either the manager or the interns (has/have) the file."

Answer: have — "interns" is the nearer subject and is plural.
4

Ignore interrupting phrases

as well as, along with, in addition to, together with

Phrases inserted between the subject and verb — including ones that sound like "and" (as well as, along with, together with, in addition to) — do not change the verb's number. Match the verb to the real subject only.

✓The manager, along with his staff, is attending.
✓The basket of apples is on the table.
✗The manager, along with his staff, are attending.

Quick check: "The captain, as well as his teammates, (was/were) praised."

Answer: was — "the captain" (singular) is the true subject; "as well as his teammates" is just an inserted phrase.
5

Indefinite pronouns

some are always singular, some always plural, some depend

Always singular: each, either, neither, one, everyone, everybody, everything, someone, somebody, anyone, no one, nobody, nothing.
Always plural: both, few, many, several, others.
Depends on the noun after "of": some, any, none, all, most, half.

✓Everyone is invited.
✓Few were chosen for the final round.
✓Some of the cake is gone. / Some of the cookies are gone.

Quick check: "Neither of the answers (is/are) correct."

Answer: is — "neither" is always singular, regardless of what follows "of."
6

Collective nouns

singular as a unit, plural when members act individually

Words like team, family, committee, staff, jury, audience, and class are singular when the group acts as one unit — which is the more common case. Use a plural verb only when you're emphasizing the individual members acting separately.

✓The team is winning the match. (acting as one unit)
✓The family are arguing among themselves about the plan. (individuals disagreeing)

Quick check: "The committee (meets/meet) every Monday."

Answer: meets — the committee is acting as a single unit.
7

"There is / There are"

the verb agrees with the noun that comes after it

In "there is / there are" sentences, "there" is not the real subject — the noun after the verb is. Look ahead before choosing is/are.

✓There is a book on the table.
✓There are three books on the table.
✗There's many reasons to stay. (should be "there are")

Quick check: "There (is/are) a lot of traffic today."

Answer: is — "traffic" is uncountable/singular here, even though "a lot of" sounds like a lot.
8

Titles & single units

plural in form, but treated as one thing

Titles of books/movies, subject names, and amounts of money, time, or distance are treated as a single unit and take a singular verb — even if they look plural.

✓"The Avengers" is a great movie.
✓Ten dollars is not enough for this.
✓Mathematics is my favorite subject.

Quick check: "Fifty kilometers (is/are) a long way to walk."

Answer: is — the distance is treated as one single amount.
9

Plural form, singular meaning

news, measles, the United States...

Some nouns end in "-s" but are grammatically singular: news, mathematics, physics, economics, athletics, measles. Country/organization names that look plural (the United States, the United Nations) are also singular — they refer to one entity.

✓The news is shocking.
✓The United States has a new trade policy.
✗The news are shocking.

Quick check: "Economics (is/are) a tough subject."

Answer: is — despite the "-s" ending, it names one field of study.
10

Relative clauses (who / which / that)

the verb agrees with the antecedent, not the nearest noun

In a clause starting with who/which/that, the verb must agree with whatever noun that pronoun refers back to (its antecedent) — which is not always the noun right in front of it.

✓She is one of the girls who play tennis. ("who" refers to "girls," plural)
✓He is the only one of the students who is late. ("who" refers to "one," singular)

Quick check: "This is one of the reasons that (make/makes) the plan risky."

Answer: make — "that" refers back to "reasons" (plural), not "one."
11

"Each" and "Every"

always singular, even before compound subjects

When "each" or "every" comes before a subject — even a compound one joined by "and" — the verb stays singular, because each item is being considered one at a time.

✓Each boy and each girl was given a gift.
✓Every man, woman, and child was affected.
✗Every student and teacher were present.

Quick check: "Every room and hallway (was/were) inspected."

Answer: was — "every" forces a singular verb.
12

Fractions & percentages

the verb agrees with the noun after "of"

With fractions, percentages, and words like "half" or "the majority," look at the noun that follows "of" — if it's uncountable or singular, use a singular verb; if plural, use a plural verb.

✓Half of the pizza is gone. (pizza → singular)
✓Half of the slices are gone. (slices → plural)
✓Two-thirds of the students are present.

Quick check: "Seventy percent of the work (is/are) done."

Answer: is — "work" is uncountable/singular.

Practice lab — mixed Subject–Verb Agreement

reveal each answer after you try

These twelve mix every rule above in random order — the way they'll actually show up in real writing and exams.

1. Neither of the boys (know/knows) the answer.

Answer: knows — "neither" is always singular.

2. The scissors (is/are) on the shelf.

Answer: are — "scissors" is one of a small group of nouns (also: trousers, glasses) that are always plural.

3. The number of students (has/have) increased this year.

Answer: has — "the number" (singular) is the subject; "a number of students" would take a plural verb instead.

4. Either the windows or the door (need/needs) fixing.

Answer: needs — agrees with the nearer subject, "the door."

5. My advisor, together with two colleagues, (is/are) reviewing my thesis.

Answer: is — "together with" doesn't change the real (singular) subject, "my advisor."

6. All of the water (has/have) evaporated.

Answer: has — "water" is uncountable.

7. The jury (has/have) reached separate opinions on the verdict.

Answer: have — emphasizes the jurors disagreeing as individuals.

8. Gymnastics (require/requires) years of practice.

Answer: requires — a plural-looking subject with a singular meaning.

9. He is one of those people who (always arrive / always arrives) late.

Answer: always arrive — "who" refers to "people" (plural).

10. Every one of my friends (was/were) at the party.

Answer: was — "every one" is always singular.

11. There (is/are) several reasons to reconsider.

Answer: are — "reasons" (plural) is the real subject after "there."

12. Twenty minutes (is/are) all I need to finish.

Answer: is — a period of time counted as one single block.
Part Two · 10 Rules

Articles — a / an / the / (no article)

Articles do one job: they tell the listener how specific a noun is. "A/an" introduces something new or general. "The" points to something already known or unique. No article at all is its own choice — used deliberately for general and proper nouns.

1

"A" vs "an" — go by sound, not spelling

the rule is about pronunciation, never the letter

Use a before a word that starts with a consonant sound, and an before a word that starts with a vowel sound — the actual spelling doesn't matter.

✓a university (starts with a "yoo" sound)
✓an hour (the "h" is silent → vowel sound)
✓a European trip / an heir to the throne

Quick check: "She's (a/an) honest, hard-working colleague."

Answer: an — "honest" begins with a silent h, so it sounds like it starts with a vowel.
2

When to use "a/an"

one, non-specific, first mention

Use a/an with a singular countable noun when: (1) it's the first time you mention it, (2) you mean "any one" rather than a specific one, or (3) you're stating someone's job/category, or a rate/quantity.

✓I saw a cat on the roof. (first mention)
✓She is a doctor. (job/category)
✓Take this medicine twice a day. (rate)

Quick check: "He wants to become (a/an) engineer."

Answer: an — stating a category/profession; "engineer" starts with a vowel sound.
3

"The" for something already known

second mention, or made specific by the sentence

Use "the" once a noun has already been introduced, or when extra words in the sentence (like "of," a clause, or context) narrow it down to one specific thing that both speaker and listener can identify.

✓I saw a cat. The cat was orange. (second mention)
✓The man who called earlier left a message. (narrowed by the clause)

Quick check: "I bought a laptop yesterday. (A/The) laptop is very fast."

Answer: The — it's now the specific laptop already mentioned.
4

"The" for things that are one-of-a-kind

the sun, the internet, the sky

Some nouns refer to something there's only one of in the shared world — use "the," never "a/an," with these.

✓The sun rises in the east.
✓We searched the internet for answers.
✗A moon looked beautiful tonight.

Quick check: " (A/The) sky turned orange at sunset."

Answer: The — there's only one sky.
5

"The" with superlatives & ordinal numbers

the best, the first, the only

Superlatives (best, tallest, most expensive) and ordinal numbers (first, second, next, last) point to one specific item out of a group — so they take "the."

✓She is the tallest student in the class.
✓This is the first time I've visited Delhi.

Quick check: "He finished (a/the) race in second place."

Answer: the — "race" here is a specific, known event.
6

"The" with geographic names

rivers, oceans, mountain ranges, plural countries

Use "the" with rivers, seas, oceans, mountain ranges, deserts, and countries whose name is plural or contains "of."

✓the Ganges, the Pacific Ocean, the Himalayas, the Sahara
✓the Netherlands, the United Kingdom, the United States
✗We rafted down Ganges last summer.

Quick check: " (Himalayas/The Himalayas) attract climbers every year."

Answer: The Himalayas — mountain ranges always take "the."
7

"The" with groups & nationalities

adjectives standing in for a whole class of people

"The" turns an adjective into a noun meaning "people of that kind" — and is used before nationality adjectives when referring to the people of that nation as a whole.

✓The elderly deserve our respect. (elderly people in general)
✓The French are known for their cuisine.

Quick check: "The government introduced new housing for (poor/the poor)."

Answer: the poor — "the" + adjective refers to the whole group of poor people.
8

Zero article — general statements

plural & uncountable nouns used generally

When you're speaking about something in general — not one specific example — use no article at all with plural or uncountable nouns.

✓Dogs are loyal animals. (dogs in general)
✓Water is essential for life.
✗The dogs are loyal animals. (wrongly implies specific dogs)

Quick check: " (The honesty/Honesty) is the best policy."

Answer: Honesty — an abstract idea spoken of in general takes no article.
9

Zero article — most proper nouns

names of people, most countries, cities, single mountains

Most proper nouns take no article: people's names, most country and city names, continents, single lakes and mountains, streets.

✓India, Chennai, Africa, Mount Everest, Lake Victoria, MG Road
✗The India has a rich history.

Quick check: " (Everest/The Everest) is the tallest mountain on Earth."

Answer: Everest — single mountain names take no article (compare: mountain ranges do take "the").
10

Zero article — transport, languages, meals, time

by car, speak Spanish, have breakfast, next week

No article before: modes of transport after "by," languages, academic subjects, sports, meals in general, and "next/last" + a time period.

✓We traveled by train. She speaks Spanish. We play cricket.
✓Breakfast is at 8. See you next week.

Quick check: "They go to work (by the bus/by bus) every day."

Answer: by bus — transport methods after "by" take no article.

Practice lab — mixed Articles

reveal each answer after you try

Choose a, an, the, or "—" (no article) for each blank.

1. I need umbrella — it looks like rain.

Answer: an — "umbrella" starts with a vowel sound; not yet specific.

2. moon was full and bright last night.

Answer: The — there's only one moon.

3. My brother plays guitar every evening.

Answer: the — musical instruments take "the" (the guitar, the piano).

4. lions are dangerous animals. (general statement)

Answer: — (no article) — a general truth about lions as a species.

5. She was born in Japan and grew up in United States.

Answer: — Japan (no article, ordinary country) ... the United States (plural-form country name).

6. This is most beautiful sunset I've ever seen.

Answer: the — superlatives take "the."

7. Could you pass me salt, please? (it's right there on the table)

Answer: the — a specific, visible item both speakers can identify.

8. He is honest man, but not always tactful one.

Answer: an honest man ... a tactful one — "honest" starts with a silent h (vowel sound), "tactful" doesn't.

9. We usually eat lunch around one o'clock.

Answer: — (no article) — meals in general take no article.

10. Taj Mahal is one of Seven Wonders of the World.

Answer: The Taj Mahal (a well-known specific monument) ... the Seven Wonders (a specific, defined group).

11. My sister wants to be architect one day.

Answer: an — "architect" starts with a vowel sound; a general profession.

12. I go to gym three times week.

Answer: the gym (a specific known place you go to) ... a week (rate — "times a week").
