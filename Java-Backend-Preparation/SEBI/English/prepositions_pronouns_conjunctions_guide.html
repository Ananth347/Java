The three words that connect everything else.

Prepositions locate things in time and space. Pronouns keep sentences from repeating themselves. Conjunctions link ideas together — and punctuate them correctly. These are the words that hold a sentence's structure up, so small mistakes here are the ones native speakers notice fastest. Each rule below has a plain-English note, correct-vs-incorrect examples, and a quick check.

Part One · 10 Rules

Prepositions

A preposition sits before a noun or pronoun and shows how it relates to the rest of the sentence — usually in time, place, or direction. English prepositions don't follow tidy logic, so the fastest route to mastery is pattern recognition, not memorized definitions alone.

1

What a preposition does

it links a noun/pronoun to the rest of the sentence

A preposition is always followed by an "object" — a noun, pronoun, or -ing form — never by a plain verb. Together, preposition + object forms a prepositional phrase that acts like an adjective or adverb.

✓The book on the shelf is mine. (on the shelf → describes "book")
✓She arrived after lunch. (after lunch → tells us when)

Quick check: In "The cat under the table is sleeping," what is the object of "under"?

Answer: "the table" — it's the noun that completes the prepositional phrase.
2

Time: at / on / in

precise point → day/date → longer period

Think of it as zooming out: at for an exact point in time, on for a day or date, in for a longer period like a month, year, or season.

✓at 6 o'clock, at noon, at midnight, at the moment
✓on Monday, on July 4th, on my birthday
✓in March, in 2024, in summer, in the morning

Quick check: "The store closes (at/on/in) 9 p.m."

Answer: at — an exact clock time.
3

Time: since / for / by / until

starting point, duration, deadline, or continuation

Since + a starting point (since 2019). For + a length of time (for two years). By + a deadline — means "no later than" (by Friday). Until/till + the point something continues up to, then stops (open until 9pm).

✓I've lived here since 2019. / I've lived here for five years.
✓Please finish the report by Friday. (deadline)
✓The shop is open until 9pm. (continues, then stops)

Quick check: "Submit your form (by/until) 5pm today."

Answer: by — a one-time deadline, not a continuing state.
4

Place: at / on / in

a point → a surface → an enclosed space

Same zoom logic as time: at for a specific point (at the door), on for a surface (on the wall), in for something enclosed or contained (in the box, in the city).

✓at the bus stop, at the corner, at 24 Elm Street
✓on the table, on the wall, on page 12
✓in the box, in Chennai, in the car

Quick check: "The picture is hanging (at/on/in) the wall."

Answer: on — a surface.
5

Place: between / among, over / under, in front of / behind

position words that come in logical pairs

Between = with exactly two things; among = within a group of three or more. Over/above = higher than; under/below = lower than. In front of and behind describe position along a line of sight.

✓She sat between her two brothers.
✓The prize was shared among the five winners.
✓The lamp hangs over the table. The cat hid under the bed.

Quick check: "The negotiations happened (between/among) the twelve member nations."

Answer: among — more than two parties involved.
6

Direction & movement

to, into, onto, through, across, towards

These describe motion rather than a fixed position: to (destination), into (entering an enclosed space), onto (moving to a surface), through (from one side to the other), across (side to side over a surface), towards (in the direction of, not necessarily arriving).

✓She walked into the room and jumped onto the bed.
✓We drove through the tunnel and across the bridge.
✓He waved and walked towards the exit.

Quick check: "The kids ran (into/onto) the swimming pool." (they entered the water)

Answer: into — entering an enclosed volume (the water), not just a surface.
7

Agent vs instrument: "by" vs "with"

who did it, vs what they used

In passive sentences, use by for the person/thing that performs the action (the agent), and with for the tool or instrument used to do it.

✓The letter was written by my grandfather. (agent)
✓The letter was written with a fountain pen. (instrument)
✗The door was opened with the thief. (should be "by the thief")

Quick check: "The cake was decorated (by/with) a piping bag."

Answer: with — the piping bag is the tool, not the person doing it.
8

Fixed pairs (dependent prepositions)

certain verbs/adjectives/nouns always pair with one preposition

Many verbs, adjectives, and nouns are followed by one specific preposition by convention — not logic. These have to be learned individually and memorized as a pair.

listen todepend onbelieve inlook forafraid of good atinterested inmarried toproud ofreason for
✓She is good at painting and interested in history.
✗He is good in painting. / She depends of her sister.

Quick check: "I'm really looking forward (to/for) the trip."

Answer: to — "look forward to" is a fixed phrasal pair.
9

Compound prepositions

multi-word prepositions that act as one unit

Several prepositions are made of more than one word but function as a single preposition: because of, in spite of, according to, instead of, due to, in addition to, on behalf of.

✓The match was cancelled because of rain.
✓In spite of the traffic, we arrived on time.
✓According to the report, sales have improved.

Quick check: " (Instead of/Because of) coffee, she ordered tea."

Answer: Instead of — shows replacement/substitution, not cause.
10

Common pitfalls

home/downtown, arrive at/in, ending a sentence with a preposition

No preposition before home, downtown, or next/last + time word (go home, not "go to home"). Use arrive at for a building/point, arrive in for a city/country. Ending a sentence with a preposition is grammatically fine and common in speech ("Who did you go with?") — formal writing simply prefers moving it to the front ("With whom did you go?").

✓I'm going home. / We arrived in Tokyo, then arrived at the hotel.
✗I'm going to home. / We arrived to Tokyo.

Quick check: "She went (—/to) home after the meeting." (fill only if needed)

Answer: no preposition — "home" already functions as an adverb here.

Practice lab — mixed Prepositions

reveal each answer after you try

Fill each blank with the correct preposition.

1. The meeting is scheduled Tuesday 10 a.m.

Answer: on Tuesday, at 10 a.m.

2. She has worked here five years, 2019.

Answer: for five years, since 2019.

3. The remote is the sofa cushions.

Answer: between (if two cushions) or among (if several) — "between" is most natural for a couch's cushions.

4. He dived the pool and swam the far end.

Answer: into the pool, towards/to the far end.

5. The novel was written a famous author, entirely hand.

Answer: by a famous author (agent), by hand (idiomatic instrument phrase).

6. I'm not very good chess, but I'm interested learning.

Answer: good at chess, interested in learning.

7. the storm, the flight departed on time.

Answer: In spite of / Despite — shows an unexpected outcome.

8. We should be there 6pm at the latest — don't be late.

Answer: by — a deadline.

9. The cat jumped the fence and vanished the bushes.

Answer: over the fence, into the bushes.

10. They finally arrived Paris after arriving the airport three hours earlier.

Answer: arrived in Paris (city), arrived at the airport (point/building).
Part Two · 10 Rules

Pronouns

Pronouns stand in for nouns already mentioned. Most pronoun errors come down to one of two things: picking the wrong case (I vs me, who vs whom) or losing track of what the pronoun is actually pointing back to.

1

Subject vs object pronouns

I/he/she/they/we do the action; me/him/her/them/us receive it

Use a subject pronoun (I, you, he, she, it, we, they) when the pronoun performs the verb's action. Use an object pronoun (me, you, him, her, it, us, them) when it receives the action or follows a preposition.

✓He and I went to the market. (both are subjects)
✓The teacher gave the prize to him and me.
✗Me and him went to the market.

Quick check: "Between you and (I/me), I think she's right."

Answer: me — it follows the preposition "between," so it must be an object pronoun.
2

Pronoun–antecedent agreement

match number and gender to the noun it replaces

A pronoun must agree with its antecedent (the noun it refers to) in number (singular/plural) and, where relevant, gender.

✓Each student must bring his or her own laptop.
✓The players collected their jerseys. (players → plural → their)
✗Every employee must submit their badge to it. ("it" doesn't match "employee")

Quick check: "The company announced (its/their) new policy." (one company)

Answer: its — "company" is a singular collective noun here.
3

The singular "they"

standard for an unknown or indefinite person

With indefinite pronouns (everyone, someone, anybody) or when a person's gender is unknown/irrelevant, "they/their/them" is now standard usage — smoother than "he or she" in most modern writing.

✓Someone left their umbrella at the door.
✓If a customer calls, ask them for their order number.

Quick check: "Everybody should bring (his/their) own lunch."

Answer: their — natural, gender-neutral modern usage.
4

Pronoun case after prepositions & comparisons

between you and me; taller than I (am)

After a preposition, always use the object form. After "than" or "as" in a comparison, mentally complete the missing verb to choose correctly — this often reveals a subject pronoun is needed.

✓This is a secret between you and me.
✓She is taller than I (am tall). / He trusts her more than me (than he trusts me).

Quick check: "My brother is older than (I/me)." (complete: "...than I am")

Answer: I — short for "older than I am," so it needs the subject form.
5

Reflexive vs intensive pronouns

myself as an object, vs myself for emphasis

A reflexive pronoun (myself, himself, themselves) is used when the subject and object are the same person. An intensive pronoun looks identical but is only for emphasis — it can be removed without changing the meaning.

✓She hurt herself while gardening. (reflexive — required)
✓I fixed it myself. (intensive — adds emphasis, could be dropped)
✗Please send the report to myself. (should be "to me")

Quick check: Is "myself" reflexive or intensive in "The director himself approved the plan"?

Answer: Intensive — it just emphasizes "the director," and could be removed.
6

"mine" vs "my" and "its" vs "it's"

possessive pronouns never take an apostrophe

A possessive adjective (my, your, his, her, its, our, their) sits before a noun. A possessive pronoun (mine, yours, his, hers, its, ours, theirs) stands alone. None of these ever take an apostrophe — "it's" is always the contraction of "it is."

✓This is my book. / This book is mine.
✓The dog wagged its tail. / It's raining outside.
✗The dog wagged it's tail.

Quick check: " (Its/It's) been a long day."

Answer: It's — short for "it has."
7

Demonstrative pronouns vs adjectives

this/that/these/those, alone or before a noun

This/that/these/those act as pronouns when they stand alone for a noun, and as adjectives when they sit directly before one. Match singular (this/that) to singular nouns, and plural (these/those) to plural nouns.

✓This is my favorite. (pronoun) / This book is my favorite. (adjective)
✓Those shoes are new. (not "them shoes")

Quick check: " (This/These) news is surprising." (news is singular)

Answer: This — "news" is grammatically singular.
8

Relative pronouns: who / whom / whose / which / that

and when their clause needs commas

Use who for a subject, whom for an object, whose for possession, which for things, and that for people or things in essential (restrictive) clauses. A restrictive clause (no commas) is essential to identify the noun; a non-restrictive clause (set off by commas) just adds extra info and uses "which," not "that."

✓The man who called is my uncle. (subject)
✓The man whom I called is my uncle. (object)
✓My car, which is ten years old, still runs well. (non-restrictive, extra info)

Quick check: "The book (that/whom) I borrowed is overdue."

Answer: that — "whom" is only for people.
9

Reciprocal pronouns: each other / one another

mutual action between two or more people

Traditionally, each other is used for exactly two people/things, and one another for three or more — though in everyday modern usage the two are often used interchangeably.

✓The two brothers helped each other move.
✓All five roommates looked out for one another.

Quick check: "The couple gave (each other/themselves) matching rings."

Answer: each other — a mutual action between the two of them, not each acting on themself.
10

Avoiding ambiguous pronoun reference

make sure "it," "they," or "this" clearly points to one noun

If a pronoun could refer to more than one noun in the sentence, the reader has to guess — rewrite to name the noun directly instead.

✗Riya told Sara that she had won. (who won — Riya or Sara?)
✓Riya told Sara, "You won."

Quick check: What's ambiguous in "When I put the laptop on the desk, it broke"?

Answer: "It" could refer to the laptop or the desk — rewrite as "the laptop broke" or "the desk broke" to remove the ambiguity.

Practice lab — mixed Pronouns

reveal each answer after you try

Choose the correct pronoun or fix the error in each sentence.

1. (Him and me / He and I) are planning the trip together.

Answer: He and I — both are subjects of "are planning."

2. Each of the girls brought (her/their) own lunch.

Answer: her — "each" is singular, and gender is known here (girls).

3. This gift is from (my sister and I / my sister and me).

Answer: my sister and me — follows the preposition "from," so object case.

4. The award went to the team (who/which) worked the hardest.

Answer: which (or "that") — "team" is treated as a thing/group here, not a person.

5. I did all the cooking (myself/me).

Answer: myself — intensive pronoun for emphasis.

6. Whose umbrella is this — is it (your's/yours)?

Answer: yours — possessive pronouns never take an apostrophe.

7. (These/This) shoes are too tight.

Answer: These — "shoes" is plural.

8. She is the one (who/whom) I trust the most.

Answer: whom — it's the object of "trust" ("I trust whom").

9. The three friends have known (each other/themselves) since childhood.

Answer: each other (or one another) — a mutual relationship among them.

10. Fix the ambiguity: "When Priya met Anu, she was nervous." Who was nervous?

Answer: Unclear — rewrite as "Priya was nervous when she met Anu" (or name Anu directly) to remove the ambiguity.
Part Three · 10 Rules

Conjunctions

Conjunctions link words, phrases, and clauses — and the type you choose decides the punctuation around it. Get the comma rules right here and your writing instantly looks more careful.

1

Coordinating conjunctions — FANBOYS

join two equal, independent elements

For, And, Nor, But, Or, Yet, So join words, phrases, or two independent (complete) clauses of equal weight. When joining two full clauses, put a comma before the conjunction.

✓I wanted to go, but it was raining.
✓She studies French and Spanish. (no comma — joins two words only)
✗I wanted to go but it was raining. (missing comma between two full clauses)

Quick check: Does this need a comma? "He was tired___ he kept working." (add "so")

Answer: Yes — "He was tired, so he kept working." Two independent clauses joined by "so" need a comma before it.
2

Subordinating conjunctions

because, although, since, if, unless, while, when...

These attach a dependent (incomplete) clause to a main clause, showing cause, contrast, condition, or time. Use a comma when the dependent clause comes first; no comma is needed when it comes second.

✓Although he was tired, he finished the race.
✓He finished the race although he was tired. (no comma needed)

Quick check: Punctuate correctly: "If it rains we will stay home"

Answer: "If it rains, we will stay home." — the "if" clause comes first, so it needs a comma.
3

Correlative conjunctions

either...or, neither...nor, both...and, not only...but also

Correlative conjunctions work in matched pairs and require parallel grammatical structure on both sides — the same part of speech or clause type after each half.

✓She is not only talented but also hardworking. (adjective / adjective)
✗She is not only talented but also works hard. (adjective / verb phrase — mismatched)

Quick check: Fix the parallelism: "He either wants to leave or staying."

Answer: "He either wants to leave or wants to stay." (or "...to leave or to stay") — matching infinitive structures.
4

Conjunctive adverbs

however, therefore, moreover, consequently — semicolon before, comma after

Words like however, therefore, moreover, furthermore, and consequently connect two independent clauses but are punctuated differently from FANBOYS: use a semicolon before and a comma after, or start a new sentence.

✓It was raining; however, we still went for a walk.
✓It was raining. However, we still went for a walk.
✗It was raining, however, we still went for a walk. (comma splice)

Quick check: Punctuate: "The plan failed___ therefore___ we need a new one"

Answer: "The plan failed; therefore, we need a new one." — semicolon before, comma after.
5

Avoiding comma splices & run-ons

two full sentences can't be joined by just a comma

A comma splice happens when two independent clauses are joined with only a comma and no conjunction. Fix it by adding a coordinating conjunction, using a semicolon, or splitting into two sentences.

✗I was hungry, I made a sandwich.
✓I was hungry, so I made a sandwich. / I was hungry; I made a sandwich.

Quick check: Fix the splice: "She loves music, she plays the violin daily."

Answer: "She loves music, and she plays the violin daily." (or split into two sentences, or use a semicolon).
6

"because" vs "because of" (conjunction vs preposition)

a full clause vs just a noun

Because is a conjunction — it's followed by a full clause with a subject and verb. Because of is a preposition — it's followed by just a noun or noun phrase. The same pattern applies to "although" (conjunction) vs "despite/in spite of" (preposition).

✓We stayed home because it was raining. (clause)
✓We stayed home because of the rain. (noun phrase)
✗We stayed home because of it was raining.

Quick check: " (Although/Despite) he was tired, he kept going."

Answer: Although — followed by a full clause ("he was tired").
7

Starting a sentence with "And" or "But"

grammatically fine — a style choice, not an error

Despite the old classroom rule, it is grammatically acceptable to begin a sentence with "and," "but," or "or," and professional writers do it often for emphasis. In very formal or academic writing, some style guides still prefer avoiding it — know your audience.

✓He knew the risks. But he took the job anyway.

Quick check: True or false — starting a sentence with "And" is always a grammar error.

Answer: False — it's a style preference, not a rule of grammar.
8

"so" vs "so that"

a result, vs a purpose

Use so (comma before) to show a result of what just happened. Use so that to show the purpose or goal behind an action — often with "can/could/would."

✓It started raining, so we went inside. (result)
✓We went inside so that we wouldn't get wet. (purpose)

Quick check: "She saved money (so/so that) she could travel." (purpose)

Answer: so that — expresses the goal behind saving.
9

"whether" vs "if"

a choice between options, vs a condition

Use whether when presenting a choice between alternatives (often with "or not"), especially in formal writing and after prepositions. Use if for a conditional situation. In casual speech the two often overlap, but "whether" is safer in formal contexts.

✓I don't know whether she is coming or not. (choice)
✓If she comes, let me know. (condition)

Quick check: "Please confirm (if/whether) you'll attend the meeting."

Answer: whether — a formal choice between attending or not, not a condition.
10

Parallelism with conjunctions

keep matched grammar on both sides of "and/or/but"

Whenever a conjunction joins a list or pair, keep the grammatical form consistent on every item — all nouns, all -ing forms, or all infinitives, never a mix.

✓She enjoys reading, writing, and painting. (all -ing forms)
✗She enjoys reading, to write, and painting.

Quick check: Fix it: "The plan is cheap, fast, and it works well."

Answer: "The plan is cheap, fast, and effective." — keep all three as matching adjectives.

Practice lab — mixed Conjunctions

reveal each answer after you try

Choose the right conjunction or fix the punctuation.

1. I wanted pizza, (but/so) my friend wanted sushi.

Answer: but — shows contrast between two preferences.

2. Fix the splice: "The store was closed, we went home."

Answer: "The store was closed, so we went home." (or use a semicolon, or split into two sentences)

3. (Neither/Either) the coach nor the players were satisfied with the result.

Answer: Neither — pairs correctly with "nor."

4. Punctuate: "The traffic was heavy however we still made it on time"

Answer: "The traffic was heavy; however, we still made it on time." — semicolon before, comma after a conjunctive adverb.

5. (Because/Because of) the heavy rain, the match was postponed.

Answer: Because of — followed by a noun phrase, not a clause.

6. She is both talented (and/but) disciplined.

Answer: and — completes the "both...and" correlative pair.

7. Fix the parallelism: "He likes swimming, to hike, and cycling."

Answer: "He likes swimming, hiking, and cycling." — all three in -ing form.

8. We left early (so/so that) we wouldn't miss the train.

Answer: so that — expresses purpose.

9. (If/Whether) you agree or not, the decision has been made.

Answer: Whether — pairs with "or not" to show a choice, regardless of outcome.

10. Add a comma if needed: "Although she was nervous she gave a great speech"

Answer: "Although she was nervous, she gave a great speech." — comma needed because the dependent clause comes first.
