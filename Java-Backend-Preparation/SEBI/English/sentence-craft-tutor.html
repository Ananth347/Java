🗺️ Why the modules are in this order

Writing is built in layers, so the course follows the same order real fluency develops in:

  1. Sentence Basics & Sentence Types — you can't build a house without knowing what a brick is. These two modules are the brick.
  2. Subject-Verb Agreement, Fragments & Run-ons, Punctuation — the "don't break the rules" layer. This is what separates a correct sentence from an incorrect one.
  3. Tenses in Writing, Active/Passive Voice, Reported Speech — control over meaning and tone. This is what separates a correct sentence from a precise one.
  4. Linking Words, Parallelism, Word Choice — the "connect your ideas smoothly" layer. This is what separates a list of correct sentences from writing that actually flows.
  5. Paragraph Coherence — zooming out from the sentence to the paragraph, which is really what essays and any longer piece of writing are built from.
If you only remember one thing: almost all "weak writing" is really just short sentences joined badly, or one wrong small word (a verb ending, a preposition, a linking word). Fixing those two things fixes 90% of what feels wrong about a piece of writing.

Module 1

Sentence Basics — Subject, Verb, Object

Every single sentence in English, no matter how long or fancy, is built from the same three jobs. Once you can spot these three in any sentence, you can build — and fix — any sentence.

The three jobs in a sentence

Subject — who or what the sentence is about.
Verb — the action, or the state of being.
Object — who or what receives the action (not every sentence needs one).

Example: The manager (subject) reviewed (verb) the report (object).

A sentence needs at minimum a subject + a verb to be complete. "She smiled." is a complete sentence all by itself — two words, one job each.

The core sentence patterns

PatternExample
Subject + Verb (S+V)Birds fly.
Subject + Verb + Object (S+V+O)She wrote a letter.
Subject + Verb + Complement (S+V+C)He is a doctor. / The soup tastes good.
Subject + Verb + Indirect Object + Direct ObjectShe gave him a book.

Almost every sentence you'll ever write is one of these four patterns, just dressed up with extra description.

Module 2

Sentence Types — Simple, Compound, Complex

Using only short, simple sentences makes writing feel childish. Using only long ones makes it exhausting to read. Good writers mix all four types on purpose.

The four types

TypeWhat it isExample
SimpleOne independent clause (one complete idea).The market crashed.
CompoundTwo independent clauses joined by and, but, or, so, yet, for, nor — or a semicolon.The market crashed, and investors panicked.
ComplexOne independent clause + one dependent clause (starts with because, although, when, if, since, while...).Although the market crashed, the company survived.
Compound-ComplexAt least two independent clauses + at least one dependent clause.Although the market crashed, the company survived, and it even grew the next year.
Independent clause = can stand alone as a full sentence. Dependent clause = starts with a word like because/although/when and cannot stand alone, even though it has a subject and verb.

Module 3

Subject-Verb Agreement

This is the single most common small mistake in Indian-English writing, and it's the fastest one to fix once you know the rules. The verb must always "agree" in number with its subject.

The core rules

  • Singular subject → singular verb. "The report needs approval."
  • Plural subject → plural verb. "The reports need approval."
  • Ignore words between subject and verb. The verb agrees with the real subject, not the nearest noun. "The list of items is long." (not "are" — list is singular, "of items" is just extra description).
  • Two subjects joined by "and" → plural verb. "Ravi and Priya are coming."
  • Two subjects joined by "or"/"nor" → verb agrees with the nearer subject. "Neither the manager nor the employees were informed."
  • Collective nouns (team, family, government) are usually singular in formal writing. "The committee has decided."
  • Indefinite pronouns like everyone, everybody, each, either, neither, nobody are singular. "Everyone is welcome."
✗ Wrong✓ Right
The list of candidates are ready.The list of candidates is ready.
Each of the students have submitted the form.Each of the students has submitted the form.
The data shows a clear pattern (informal use).The data show a clear pattern. (formally, "data" is plural)

Module 4

Fixing Fragments & Run-on Sentences

These are the two opposite mistakes: a fragment gives you less than a full sentence; a run-on crams too many full sentences together with no proper joint.

Sentence fragment

A fragment looks like a sentence (starts with a capital, ends with a period) but is missing a subject, a verb, or a complete thought — often it's really just a dependent clause left on its own.

✗ Fragment✓ Fixed
Because the market crashed.Because the market crashed, investors panicked.
Running to catch the train.She was running to catch the train.

Run-on sentence (and the comma splice)

A run-on jams two complete sentences together with no punctuation at all. A comma splice is the sneakier cousin — it joins two complete sentences with only a comma, which isn't strong enough to do that job alone.

✗ Run-on / Comma splice✓ Fixed (three valid ways)
The report was late the manager was upset.The report was late, so the manager was upset.
It started raining, we stayed inside.It started raining; we stayed inside.  /  It started raining, so we stayed inside.  /  It started raining. We stayed inside.
To fix a run-on or comma splice, you have four honest options: (1) split it into two sentences with a period, (2) add a comma + a joining word (and, but, so...), (3) use a semicolon, or (4) turn one clause into a dependent clause (because, although...).

Module 5

Punctuation Essentials

Punctuation isn't decoration — each mark tells the reader exactly how to read the sentence. Get these few marks right and your writing instantly looks more careful and professional.

The marks that matter most

  • Comma (,) — separates items in a list, sets off an introductory phrase ("After the meeting, we left."), and joins two independent clauses together with a conjunction (and, but, so...).
  • Semicolon (;) — joins two closely related independent clauses without a conjunction. "The report was late; the client was unhappy."
  • Colon (:) — introduces a list, an explanation, or an example, after a complete sentence. "She had one goal: to finish on time."
  • Apostrophe (') — shows possession (the manager's desk) or a contraction (it's = it is). Never use it just to make a word plural.
  • Quotation marks (" ") — enclose someone's exact words.
✗ Wrong✓ Right
The manager's are on leave. (plural, no apostrophe needed)The managers are on leave.
Its a good report.It's a good report. (it's = it is)
I need three things pen, paper, ink.I need three things: pen, paper, ink.

Module 6

Choosing the Right Tense in Writing

Knowing tense forms is one skill; picking the right one while writing a real paragraph is another. This module is about that second skill — and about staying consistent.

Two habits that fix most tense mistakes

  • Stay in one "time zone" unless you have a reason to switch. If your paragraph is about the past, keep describing things in the past. Don't drift into present tense mid-story without a reason.
  • For descriptive and factual writing (essays, reports, definitions), Present Simple and Past Simple do almost all the work. Present Simple states facts and general truths ("Inflation reduces purchasing power"); Past Simple narrates events that happened and finished ("The policy was introduced in 2016").
Quick check: if you're describing something generally true or currently the case, use Present Simple. If you're narrating something that already happened and is over, use Past Simple. Don't overthink it beyond that for most writing.

Module 7

Active vs. Passive Voice

This one small choice changes how confident and direct your writing sounds. Both are correct English — but overusing passive voice is the #1 reason writing feels vague or weak.

The difference

Active voice: the subject does the action. "The committee approved the budget."

Passive voice: the subject receives the action; the doer is de-emphasized or dropped entirely. "The budget was approved (by the committee)."

Formula for passive: object of the active sentence + be (is/was/has been...) + past participle + "by ..." (optional).

Prefer active voice — it's shorter, clearer, and more confident. Use passive on purpose when the doer is unknown, unimportant, or you want to emphasize the receiver of the action ("The building was damaged in the storm" — we don't need to say "the storm damaged the building").

Passive (weaker)Active (stronger)
A decision was made by the board.The board made a decision.
Mistakes were made.We made mistakes.

Module 8

Direct vs. Indirect (Reported) Speech

Whenever you write what someone said — in a report, a summary, or a precis — you'll almost always convert it into reported speech. It follows one dependable pattern.

The core shift: tense moves one step back

Direct speech→ Reported speech
"I am tired," she said.She said (that) she was tired.
"I will come," he said.He said (that) he would come.
"I have finished," they said.They said (that) they had finished.
"I went home," she said.She said (that) she had gone home.
Present → Past. Past → Past Perfect. will → would. can → could. Pronouns and time words also shift: "today" → "that day", "tomorrow" → "the next day", "here" → "there".

Module 10

Parallel Structure

When you list or compare things in a sentence, each item needs to be in the same grammatical form. This is a small rule that makes a huge difference to how polished writing sounds.

The rule

If one item in a list is a verb+ing, every item should be. If one is a noun, every item should be. Mixing forms makes a sentence feel "off" even if the reader can't say exactly why.

✗ Not parallel✓ Parallel
She likes reading, to write, and painting.She likes reading, writing, and painting.
The job requires patience, being organized, and to communicate well.The job requires patience, organization, and good communication.
He not only finished the report but also the presentation was prepared by him.He not only finished the report but also prepared the presentation.

Word Choice & Common Confusions

A handful of word pairs get mixed up constantly — in speech it barely matters, but in writing, one wrong word can change your meaning or just look careless.

The usual suspects

PairHow to tell them apart
their / there / they'retheir = belongs to them; there = a place; they're = they are
affect / effectaffect = verb (to influence); effect = noun (a result)
your / you'reyour = belongs to you; you're = you are
its / it'sits = belongs to it; it's = it is / it has
then / thanthen = time/sequence; than = comparison
fewer / lessfewer = countable things (fewer books); less = uncountable (less time)
lay / lielay = to put something down (takes an object); lie = to recline (no object)

Module 12

Paragraph Coherence

Zoom out one level. A paragraph is really just sentences working as a team — and a team needs a captain (the topic sentence) and players who all stay on message.

What makes a paragraph hold together

  • One paragraph, one main idea. If a sentence doesn't support the paragraph's main idea, it belongs in a different paragraph — or nowhere.
  • Topic sentence first (usually). It states the paragraph's main idea, so the reader knows what to expect before the details arrive.
  • Supporting sentences give evidence, examples, or explanation for the topic sentence — in a logical order (often: general → specific, or cause → effect, or chronological).
  • A concluding or transition sentence often wraps up the idea or bridges to the next paragraph.
This is exactly why Module 9 (Linking Words) matters so much here — the words that connect sentences within a paragraph are the same tools that make the whole paragraph feel like one connected thought instead of a list of facts.
